ABSTRACT
Temporal audio grounding requires locating every occurrence of a
queried target, making both occurrence counts and interval bound-
aries essential. We propose RefTAG, a post-training framework for
verifiable temporal self-correction. Its Reflection Chain-of-Thought
(Re-CoT) protocol combines draft intervals, discrete count and
boundary actions, and revised intervals in one response. Supervised
fine-tuning on these trajectories (Re-CoT-SFT) teaches the protocol;
reinforcement learning (RL) jointly rewards final localization and
revision outcomes. Across 8,228 queries, Re-CoT-SFT improves
query-weighted mIoU over final-answer SFT by 1.74 percentage
points. RL further improves TAG-Bench multi-target mIoU by
0.40 points and R@0.5 by 0.86 points. The final model achieves
38.50% TAG-Bench mIoU and 51.01% mean mIoU across three
datasets, outperforming the evaluated open-source MOSS-Audio
and FireRedAudio models and the commercial Gemini-3.1-Pro on
both measures. These results demonstrate that explicitly coupling
count- and boundary-aware revision with joint reward optimiza-
tion provides an effective and verifiable path to temporal audio
grounding.
Index Terms— temporal audio grounding, chain-of-thought,
self-reflection, reinforcement learning
1. INTRODUCTION
Temporal audio grounding (TAG) connects natural-language de-
scriptions to the intervals in which the described content occurs [1].
Unlike clip-level recognition, it requires semantic identification and
temporal evidence: when a target starts, when it ends, and whether it
recurs. Under TAG-Bench’s all-occurrence setting [2], one plausible
interval is insufficient for a target appearing in several disjoint
regions. Grounding therefore requires a variable-size interval set
with accurate coverage and boundaries, providing evidence that can
be inspected against the recording.
Large audio-language models (LALMs), including Audio
Flamingo 3 [3] and Qwen3-Omni [4], support natural-language
audio interaction. Temporal extensions address specific bottlenecks:
TimeAudio introduces temporal representations [5], TimePro-RL
combines time prompts with temporal-reward optimization [6], and
LAT-Audio retrieves local evidence through iterative tool use [7].
These methods improve temporal information access or localiza-
tion supervision. Our focus is the prediction process that follows:
identifying and correcting errors in an already generated interval set.

Temporal correction has two coupled difficulties. First, occur-
rence and boundary errors require different interventions: shifting
a boundary does not recover an omitted occurrence, while adding
intervals does not repair inaccurate endpoints. TAG-Bench reports
under-counting on recurring targets [2]; event-presence analyses also
find reduced reliability in scenes with more events [8]. Second, a
fluent critique does not measure localization improvement. Final-
answer supervision alone cannot distinguish useful revision from
preservation of an accurate draft. Two incorrect predictions can also
differ in overlap, count, and boundary accuracy. Reflection must
therefore specify the correction and quantify its effect.
These observations motivate reflection over interval predictions
rather than unrestricted explanations. Building on reflection-aware
training [9], we expose a draft, diagnose count and boundary errors,
and evaluate the revision against the same temporal criterion. This
connects the intermediate response to the grounding objective with-
out a free-text reflection judge.
We propose RefTAG with the Reflection Chain-of-Thought (Re-
CoT) protocol. Each response contains draft intervals, discrete count
and boundary actions, and revised intervals. Supervised fine-tuning
(SFT) teaches this structure; reflection-aware Group Relative Policy
Optimization (GRPO) rewards final quality and revision outcomes,
including preservation of accurate drafts. A shared quality function
combines coverage, interval matching, midpoint accuracy, and count
agreement. The backbone is unchanged, and inference requires no
annotations.
Our contributions are the structured correction protocol and the
interval-based reflection objective. Experiments separate final accu-
racy, draft informativeness, and revision gain, then test reward mix-
ing and individual quality components. Together, these comparisons
establish the protocol’s grounding gains and identify the additional
benefits of reflection-aware reinforcement learning.
