Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) generally rely
on sampling temperature to regulate the amount
of randomness in their outputs. Mainstream
methods for selecting temperature typically in-
volve fixed, heuristic global settings and con-
text or token-level calibration, rather than a
geometrically interpretable representation of
the communicative scenario. In this work, we
introduce a simple and interpretable tempera-
ture selection framework, the Scenario-Actor,
which adapts decoding to the inferred situa-
tional characteristics. Scenario-Actor extracts
scenario-relevant structures from Transformer
hidden states and determines the temperature
from scenario embedding, a structured geomet-
ric representation of the communicative set-
ting. Empirical results show that on Llama-3.1-
8B-Instruct, Scenario-Actor achieves strong
goal-completion performance in interactive so-
cial scenarios, significant gains on open-ended
instruction following, and competitive perfor-
mance on multi-turn conversation compared to
multiple baselines.
1 Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) generate text by
repeatedly sampling from a conditional next-token
distribution. One of the simplest yet most conse-
quential hyperparameters in language generation is
the sampling temperature, which controls the sharp-
ness of the next-token probability distribution and,
in turn, the rigor and creativity of model output.
Existing systems typically set it globally or adapt
it according to predictive uncertainty, context, or
learned decoding objectives, which implicitly treat
decoding primarily as a property of the model’s pre-
dictive state (Dhuliawala et al., 2024; Wang et al.,
2020). But socially situated language generation
exposes a different source of variation: the inter-
action itself. A prompt conveys not only seman-
tics but also implies a communicative setting. For
example, the conversation could be either an aca-
demic discussion or a friendly chitchat, whose de-
sired responses are either rigorous or open-ended,
formal or casual. The optimal temperature is then
circumstantial, varying across scenarios.

In our controlled experiment, we decode 245
AgentSense (Mou et al., 2025) social interactions
with four fixed temperatures. We found that first,
changing temperature substantially alters interac-
tion quality, moving between mid-range tempera-
tures flips 16 to 17% of individual goal outcomes;
second, globally strongest temperature setting does
not dominate across interactions.
