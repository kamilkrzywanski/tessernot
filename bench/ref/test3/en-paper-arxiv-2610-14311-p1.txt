Active learning has been shown to be more beneficial for student learning than didactic lecturing. Conse-
quently, researchers have developed a slate of active learning strategies for use in physics instruction, including
course-level reforms that are widely known by specific names. To increase adoption and support prolonged
implementation of these named active learning methods, it is important to understand the backgrounds and ex-
periences of instructors who are already using them. In this study, we interviewed 20 introductory physics and
astronomy instructors who use one of Student-Centered Active Learning Environment with Upside-Down Peda-
gogies (SCALE-UP), Investigative Science Learning Environment (ISLE), Tutorials in Introductory Physics and
Astronomy, or Peer Instruction to gain insight into their teaching experiences, professional development, path-
ways to learning about their method, and supports for and difficulties in their implementation. We find for our
sample that all implementers are involved with the Discipline-Based Education Research (DBER) community
(e.g., conducting DBER work, engaging with DBER researchers or content, receiving training for evidence-
based instructional practices), but there is variation in the extent of engagement with DBER across instructors.
While instructors identify a variety of supports for their implementation, such as from the broader education
community and departmental or institutional administration, the difficulties they experience are representa-
tive of both long-standing issues associated with active learning adoption (e.g., physical classroom and timing
constraints, student resistance) and new issues related to emergent technologies (e.g., Artificial Intelligence).
Finally, we uncover differences in implementers across the four named methods, particularly related to their
training for the method and the supports they received for implementation. Together, these findings underscore
the importance of supporting multiple dissemination avenues for named active learning methods, point to a pos-
sible need for more training opportunities for SCALE-UP and ISLE in particular, and identify nascent issues in
the contemporary active learning classroom that future work may further characterize and address.
I. INTRODUCTION
The use of active learning in undergraduate science instruc-
tion has well-documented benefits to students across a range
of outcomes, including conceptual learning, self-efficacy, and
retention [1–6]. Over the past decade, instructors have in-
creasingly implemented active learning, including adoption
of these strategies across different educational levels and ge-
ographic locations [7–11]. Despite the growing use of active
learning, traditional lecturing is still common [10, 11]. As
a result, there have been widespread calls for instructors to
pivot from more traditional, didactic ways of teaching to ac-
tive learning strategies [1, 12, 13].
To promote adoption of active learning, research has started
to unpack instructor pathways to using active learning strate-
gies and the supports for and barriers to implementation (e.g.,
Refs. [7, 9, 14–17]). This body of work, however, is some-
times limited to single institutions, single courses, and/or sin-
gle active learning strategies, making it difficult to draw broad
conclusions about how and why active learning adoption oc-
curs [9, 14, 18–21]. In other cases, studies span many instruc-
tors and implementations, but lack sufficient detail about the
institutional contexts and active learning strategies being used
to devise targeted implications for dissemination and adop-
tion [15, 18, 22, 23]. Studies that both capture a range of
institutional contexts and implementations and contain suffi-
cient information about the instructors and their contexts are

In physics, named active learning methods offer useful case
studies for this type of work. Physics education researchers
have developed a range of active learning techniques over
the past three decades, from single clicker questions to unit-
length interventions to these course-level reforms that are
known by specific names (e.g., Peer Instruction [24]). Such
named methods are rigorously validated by the developers, of-
ten through quasi-experimental procedures, and have demon-
strated gains in student learning [25–27]. Named methods
also carry a well-defined developmental lineage (e.g., because
they are often motivated by specific theories of learning),
structure, and set of supporting resources (e.g., the Peer In-
struction textbook [24]). While individual implementations of
the same named method may vary [28, 29], the shared name,
structure, and goals allow them to be compared across imple-
mentations and institutions.
In this study, therefore, we closely examine the dissem-
ination and use of four named active learning methods in
physics and astronomy instruction by conducting interviews
with 20 instructors using one of these methods. Several other
named active learning methods for introductory physics ex-
ist (e.g., Modeling Instruction [30] and Context-Rich Prob-
lems [31, 32]), but we limit the current study to the following
methods because they are both well-known and widely imple-
mented [10], and we aimed to collect data from multiple im-
plementations of each method (the methods are ordered from
most to least structural reform required):
